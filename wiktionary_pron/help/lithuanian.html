Lithuanian Pronunciation and IPA Transcription Guide

← Back to Lithuanian transcription ↑ All Languages

Mark the stress and tone yourself, and type in lowercase. When a word is not in the dictionary, the rule engine has no idea where the stress falls. A plain word comes out with no stress mark, no tone and no vowel length (and with syllable dots left in): labas /ɫɐ.bɐs/. To get stress, type an accent mark on the stressed vowel: a tilde (ã) means stressed and long, an acute (á) means stressed with the acute/circumflex tone mark, and a grave (à) means stressed and short. For example lãbas /ˈɫaː.bɐs/. Use precomposed letters or combining marks, both work. Vowel length is marked with ą ę į ų ū ė y, not with accents. Capital letters break the rule engine (Kauno /k.ɐʊ̯.noː/), so write kauno /kɐʊ̯.noː/. See Stress and Tone Marks and Common Issues.

About This Tool

This Lithuanian transcription app uses the Wiktionary Lithuanian Pronunciation Module to generate phonemic transcriptions for Lithuanian text. IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) is a standardized system for transcribing speech sounds. This tool converts Lithuanian spelling into IPA, helping learners, linguists and developers see how a word is pronounced.

Every word goes through two steps:

  1. Dictionary lookup. The app first looks the word up in a lexicon built from a Wiktionary data dump[1][2] (lt_lexicon.zip, about 12,600 headwords, downloaded once, decompressed in your browser and cached in IndexedDB). If the word is found, the dictionary IPA is shown. These entries come from Wiktionary editors, normally include a stress mark, and may list several variants separated by commas. If the first lookup fails, the lookup is retried with the word in lowercase.
  2. Rule-based fallback. If the word is not in the lexicon (and the lexicon is only a small fraction of Lithuanian vocabulary, so this happens often), the app calls the Lua pronunciation module: window.lt_ipa.toIPA(cleanText, true). The module's toIPA function takes exactly one parameter (text), so the second argument true is accepted and silently ignored; it changes nothing. Everything this page says about the rule engine therefore applies regardless of that argument.

Important: the examples on this page were produced with the rule engine only (the test harness does not load the lexicon). A word shown here with a stress-less, dotted result may look different in the app if the dictionary contains it. Dictionary results are generally more reliable than rule output, especially for stress, which the rules cannot guess.

Before either step the app removes everything except letters, combining marks, apostrophes and hyphens from the word (sanitize() in utils.js) and normalizes it to NFKC.

Dialects & Limitations

  • Supported: Standard Lithuanian only; the single style is Default.
  • Not supported: Regional dialects (for example Samogitian) and informal speech.
  • No stress guessing: the rules never decide which syllable is stressed. Only accent marks you type (or a dictionary entry) produce a stress mark.
  • Lowercase only: the rules look for lowercase letters; capitals confuse them (see Common Issues).
  • Not implemented in the module: voicing assimilation (dubti /dʊbʲ.tʲɪ/ keeps its b voiced), final devoicing, and any special treatment of mixed diphthongs (an, am, al, ar, ir ...). The output is a broad transcription of letters, not of connected speech.
  • Loanwords and names: letters outside the native alphabet (f, h, x, q, w) and the digraphs dž/ts are handled only partially.

Phonemic vs Phonetic

  • Phonemic (the only form offered): a broad transcription. Dictionary entries are phonemic by nature. The rule engine also emits a few predictable variants (dark ɫ, velar ŋ, palatalized consonants, ɕ ʑ) because they are built into the module, but it does not produce narrow phonetic detail such as aspiration or assimilation.

Quick Reference (Glossary)

Phoneme
The smallest unit of sound that distinguishes meaning (for example /p/ vs /b/ in pãtas vs batas).
Allophone
A variant pronunciation of a phoneme that does not change meaning, such as dark ɫ vs clear lʲ.
Palatalization
A consonant pronounced with the middle of the tongue raised toward the hard palate, written ʲ. In Lithuanian most consonants are soft before i, e and the i-digraphs, e.g. liepa /lʲɪə.pɐ/.
Soft / Hard consonant
A palatalized consonant (tʲ) versus a plain one (t). Before the vowels a, o, u, ų and ū consonants are hard, for example tavo /tɐ.ʋoː/.
Ogonek
The small hook under ą, ę, į, ų. These letters mark long vowels.
Macron
The bar over ū (a long u). The letter ė carries a dot above and is a long e.
Tone / Pitch accent
Lithuanian stressed syllables carry a tone, written with an acute (á), grave (à) or tilde (ã) accent. This tool converts the three marks into a stress sign, vowel length and, for the acute, a circumflex sign on the vowel.
Diphthong
A single-syllable vowel glide, e.g. kaina /kɐɪ.nɐ/, draugas /drɐʊ̯.ɡɐs/.
Schwa
The neutral vowel ə. In this module it appears only as the second element of the diphthongs ie and uo, e.g. siena /sʲɪə.nɐ/.
Affricate
A stop followed by a fricative, written with a tie bar: t͡s (c), t͡ʃ/t͡ɕ (č), d͡z (dz).
Velarization (dark l)
A back-of-the-tongue ɫ, used for l before hard vowels and consonants: lapas /ɫɐ.pɐs/.
Syllable Boundary
The dot (.) between syllables. This module leaves the dots in its output, so you will see them in rule-generated IPA.
Tie Bar
The mark ◌͡◌ joining two symbols into one affricate.
Lexicon
The dictionary of ready-made IPA transcriptions, consulted before the rules.
NFD
Unicode decomposed form: accented letters are split into a base letter plus combining marks so the module can match the marks.

Interactive Features

  • Click words to cycle variants: Some lexicon entries list more than one pronunciation (separated by commas). Click a transcribed word to cycle through them.
  • Audio playback: Click the speaker icon next to a word or line to hear text-to-speech (language code lt-LT; needs browser or Edge TTS voice support).
  • Export results: Use the PDF or CSV buttons to save transcriptions.

Multiple Pronunciation Variants

  • Lexicon entries: a dictionary headword can carry several transcriptions (for example different stress placements).
  • Lexicon vs. rules: the dictionary and the rule engine can give different results for the same word; the dictionary wins whenever it has the word.
  • Rule engine: always returns exactly one result per word.

Lithuanian Pronunciation Guide

This guide describes how the module turns Lithuanian spelling into sounds. Lithuanian spelling is fairly regular, but several things are not written in ordinary text: the stress position and the tone. The module can only reproduce them if you mark them.

Vowel Length & Quality

Length is carried by the letter itself: a e i u are short, while ą ę į ų ū ė y are long and o is always long. The exception is ą, which the module leaves unconverted (see Common Issues).

LetterShortLongShort ExampleLong Example
a / ą/ɐ//aː/ (only via ã)batas /bɐ.tɐs/rãtas /ˈraː.tɐs/
e / ė / ę/ɛ//eː/, /æː/medis /mʲɛ.dʲɪs/tėvas /tʲeː.ʋɐs/, ęsti /æː.sʲtʲɪ/
i / į / y/ɪ//iː/tiltas /tʲɪɫ.tɐs/jį /jiː/, sakyti /sɐ.kʲiː.tʲɪ/
u / ū / ų/ʊ//uː/kur /kʊr/rūkas /ruː.kɐs/, rankų /rɐŋ.kuː/
o(none)/oː/-kodas /koː.dɐs/

Short vowels written with the ordinary letter stay short even with a tilde (ẽ ĩ ũ), and the vowel after a soft consonant is fronted: ʲɐ becomes ʲɛ (tokia /toː.kʲɛ/), and a long aː becomes ʲæː (niãrti /ˈnʲæːrʲ.tʲɪ/).

Stress and Tone Marks

The module reads three accent marks. Per its own source comment: acute = stressed + circumflex tone, tilde = stressed + long, grave = stressed + short. A stress sign ˈ is placed before the syllable that contains the mark. Without any mark there is no stress sign at all.

MarkNo markAcute (á)Tilde (ã)Grave (à)
alapas /ɫɐ.pɐs/lába /ˈɫâː.bɐ/lãbas /ˈɫaː.bɐs/màma /ˈmɐ.mɐ/
odovana /doː.ʋɐ.nɐ/dóvana /ˈdôː.ʋɐ.nɐ/dõvana /ˈdoː.ʋɐ.nɐ/dòvana /ˈdoː.ʋɐ.nɐ/
ėmėsa /mʲeː.sɐ/mė́sa /ˈmʲêː.sɐ/mė̃sa /ˈmʲeː.sɐ/mė̀sa /ˈmʲeː.sɐ/
  • The acute turns the vowel into a circumflex-marked long vowel only for a, o, ė, ę, y, ū, ų, į (written âː, ôː ...). On a plain short e i u the module does not rewrite the acute and a stray acute stays in the output.
  • The tilde adds length only to a; on e i u it just marks stress: kẽpti /ˈkʲɛpʲ.tʲɪ/.
  • The stress sign takes the place of the syllable dot: kartà /kɐrˈtɐ/ vs kárta /ˈkâːr.tɐ/ vs kartã /kɐrˈtaː/.
  • Every syllable with a mark gets its own ˈ: dúonà /ˈdʊ̂əˈnɐ/.
  • Macron and breve are not stress marks (ūkis /uː.kʲɪs/ vs ū̃kis /ˈuː.kʲɪs/).

Palatalization (Soft Consonants)

The module marks a consonant as soft (ʲ) in these cases:

  • Before i, e, ė, ę, į, y and j-vowel combinations: medis /mʲɛ.dʲɪs/, gerti /ɡʲɛrʲ.tʲɪ/, tėvas /tʲeː.ʋɐs/. The letter y is in this list too, which is a bug for real Lithuanian (see Issues).
  • Before the i-digraphs ia, io, iu: the i disappears and only the softness remains: žiema /ʑɪə.mɐ/, čiobrelis /t͡ɕoː.bʲrʲɛ.lʲɪs/, giesmė /ɡʲɪə.sʲmʲeː/. Exception: iau keeps its i and becomes a diphthong: kiaulė /kʲɛʊ.lʲeː/.
  • Consonant clusters: a soft consonant makes the consonants before it soft too: sveikas /sʲʋʲɛɪ.kɐs/, tìlti /ˈtʲɪlʲ.tʲɪ/, anglis /ɐŋʲ.ɡʲlʲɪs/. The exception is k, which never takes ʲ this way (krienas /krʲɪə.nɐs/ and apniūkti /ɐpʲ.nʲuːk.tʲɪ/ keep a plain k, and so does the k in skaityti /skɐɪ.tʲiː.tʲɪ/).
  • After š and ž: a soft ʃ becomes ɕ and a soft ʒ becomes ʑ: šeši /ɕɛ.ɕɪ/, žemė /ʑɛ.mʲeː/; č before a soft vowel becomes t͡ɕ: čekis /t͡ɕɛ.kʲɪs/.
  • Not palatalized: consonants before a, o, u: tavo /tɐ.ʋoː/. The consonant j is never softened.

A missing j is added before an initial ie and between a vowel and ie: ieva /jɪə.ʋɐ/, paieška /pɐ.jɪə.ʃkɐ/.

Diphthongs

The module recognizes the following diphthongs (the acute-mark inputs below are illustrative). A tilde on the second vowel is dropped (it marks stress, which the stress step has already handled); an acute adds a circumflex mark on the first element.

LettersUnmarkedWith acuteNotes
aikaina /kɐɪ.nɐ/káina /ˈkɐ̂ɪ.nɐ/
audraugas /drɐʊ̯.ɡɐs/áuga /ˈɐ̂ʊ.ɡɐ/
eieiti /ɛɪ.tʲɪ/éiti /ˈɛ̂ɪ.tʲɪ/
iaukiauras /kʲɛʊ.rɐs/kiáuras /ˈkʲæ̂ʊ.rɐs/The i only softens the consonant
iemiestas /mʲɪə.stɐs/píeva /ˈpʲɪ̂ə.ʋɐ/The second element is a schwa

Limitation: diphthongs are only matched inside a word. At the end of a word the syllable-division step splits them into two syllables, so the diphthong rules do not fire: vaikai /ʋɐɪ.kɐ.ɪ/, vanduo /ʋɐn.dʊ.oː/, kaip /kɐ.ɪp/. Compare the same diphthong in the middle of a word: vaikas /ʋɐɪ.kɐs/. Words like this are the ones most likely to be better served by the dictionary.

Consonant Rules

  • g, h, c, v, q: g becomes the IPA ɡ (draugas /drɐʊ̯.ɡɐs/); h becomes ɣ (hobis /ɣ.oː.bʲɪs/); c becomes t͡s (ciklas /t͡sʲɪ.kɫɐs/); v becomes ʋ (vasara /ʋɐ.sɐ.rɐ/); q becomes k.
  • Digraphs: ch becomes x (chaosas /xɐoː.sɐs/); dz becomes d͡z (dzeta /d͡zʲɛ.tɐ/); č becomes t͡ʃ (čaga /t͡ʃɐ.ɡɐ/). ts and dž are only half supported: see Common Issues.
  • Velar nasal: n followed by k or ɡ becomes ŋ: bankas /bɐŋ.kɐs/, langas /ɫɐŋ.ɡɐs/. A soft n stays soft: anglis /ɐŋʲ.ɡʲlʲɪs/.
  • Dark l: every l that is not soft becomes ɫ: kalnas /kɐɫ.nɐs/, but not at the very end of a word: atgal /ɐt.ɡɐl/.
  • Voicing: not modified. Consonants keep their letter-based voicing even in clusters and at the end of a word.

Syllable Division

Syllable dots are left in the final output. The division rule puts a dot before each onset-plus-vowel group, treats stop + sonorant (e.g. br, pl) and s/z + stop as one onset (but splits stop + m/n, e.g. ritmas /rʲɪt.mɐs/), and keeps a vowel sequence together unless it is at the end of the word. Examples: draugas /drɐʊ̯.ɡɐs/, sluoksnis /sɫʊək.sʲnʲɪs/, aštuoni /ɐ.ʃtʊə.nʲɪ/. The letters h f x and q are not recognized as onsets, so they get a stray dot (see Common Issues).

Using the Tool: Practical Notes

How do I mark stress and tone for a word that is not in the dictionary?

Type an accent on the stressed vowel: an acute (á) gives stress with a circumflex tone sign, a tilde (ã) gives stress and length, and a grave (à) gives stress and a short vowel. The three words kartà /kɐrˈtɐ/, kartã /kɐrˈtaː/ and kárta /ˈkâːr.tɐ/ differ only in the mark. See Stress and Tone Marks. Open in the converter

Why do some words show several transcriptions?

A lexicon entry can list more than one pronunciation, for example different stress placements; click the word to cycle through them. The rule engine always returns one result. See Multiple Pronunciation Variants.

Why can the result differ from the examples on this page?

The dictionary is consulted first and wins whenever it has the word. It covers about 12,600 headwords, so many words fall through to the rules, and the examples here were generated by the rules alone. See About This Tool.

Why does the output contain dots, and why do capital letters cause errors?

The rule engine leaves syllable boundaries in its output as dots. It matches lowercase letters only, so a capital leaves a stray dot: Kauno /k.ɐʊ̯.noː/ against kauno /kɐʊ̯.noː/. See Common Issues & Limitations.

Implementation Details (for Developers)

The module is lua_modules/lt-pron_wasm.lua (216 lines of Lua, adapted from Wiktionary's Module:lt-pron). It is much shorter than the German module: there is no stress assignment, no morphology and no lexicon logic inside it. The public function used by the app is export.toIPA(text), which is exactly:

pron(ulower(export.syllabify(export.respell(text))))

So the pipeline is respell (spelling to a half-phonological notation), then syllabify (insert dots), then lowercase, then pron (notation to IPA). Because lowercasing happens after respell and syllabify, those two steps only recognize lowercase letters. The module also calls print() after respell and after syllabify (and once at load time), so the intermediate forms appear in the console (for example in smoke tests); they are debug output, not the result. The tables below show these real intermediate forms.

  1. Application layer (scripts/utils.js): sanitize, lexicon lookup, then rule fallback.
  2. Respelling (export.respell): decomposition, digraph placeholders, added j, softness marks.
  3. Syllabification (export.syllabify): dots between syllables.
  4. Pronunciation (local function pron): consonants, allophones, stress, diphthongs, single vowels, tones.

3. Syllabification (export.syllabify)

One global pattern inserts a dot before each onset+vowel group. The onset may contain s|z, a stop (ptkbdðþgçćc), a sonorant (lmnrvj), each optionally followed by caron or ʲ. Vowels and marks before the dot are carried along. Then three clean-up steps run.

4. Pronunciation (pron)

Input is the lowercased, syllabified string. Substitutions are applied in this order (the order matters: for example the diphthongs run before the single vowels).

Common Issues & Limitations

Known Transcription Problems

This table shows known issues in the rule-based path (words not in the dictionary). "Correct IPA" is the engine output for a corrected input where one exists, otherwise it is described in words:

labas labas /ɫɐ.bɐs/ lãbas /ˈɫaː.bɐs/ Unmarked input has no stress or tone information Type the accent mark on the stressed vowel (tilde, acute or grave)

Kauno Kauno /k.ɐʊ̯.noː/ kauno /kɐʊ̯.noː/ Respell/syllabify only match lowercase; the capital leaves a stray dot Type words in lowercase
džiaugsmas džiaugsmas /ð̌ʲɛʊɡ.smɐs/ þiaugsmas /d͡ʑɛʊɡ.smɐs/ The dž placeholder is never converted and leaks into the output as ð̌ Type þ in place of dž
