About This Tool

This Portuguese transcription app uses the Wiktionary Portuguese Pronunciation Module (pt-pronunc, shipped here as lua_modules/pt-pron_wasm.lua) to generate phonetic and phonemic IPA transcriptions for Portuguese text. IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) is a standardized system for transcribing speech sounds. Phonemic transcription represents the abstract sound system (phonemes), while phonetic transcription shows actual speech variations (allophones).

Unlike several other languages in this app, Portuguese is purely rule-based: there is no lexicon and no dictionary lookup. Every word is transcribed from its spelling by the Wiktionary module, which was designed for editors who type a respelling (for example cinêma) whenever the ordinary spelling does not determine the pronunciation. The consequence: where Portuguese spelling is genuinely ambiguous (most notably a stressed e or o, and the letter x), the engine refuses to guess and the app shows an error instead of a possibly wrong answer. See Important Note: Why You Might See an Error.

Dialects & Limitations

The app offers two dialect choices. Behind them, the app calls the module as window.pt_ipa.IPA(word, region) with region = "rio" for Brazil and region = "pt" for Portugal.

  • Brazil means specifically the Rio de Janeiro (carioca) profile (rio), not a generic "Brazilian" accent. What that implies in practice:
    • Syllable-final (coda) s/z is ʃ/ʒ (the chiado): pastas /ˈpaʃ.tɐʃ/. Other Brazilian accents keep s there; those profiles are not selectable here.
    • The "strong r" (rr, initial r, coda r) is ʁ phonemically and a voiceless χ phonetically: carro /ˈka.ʁu/ carro [ˈka.χu].
    • t/d before i become affricates: cidade /siˈda.d͡ʒi/.
    • Coda l is w: animal /a.niˈmaw/.
    • Unstressed final e, o, a are i, u, ɐ: bonito /boˈni.tu/, casa /ˈka.zɐ/.
  • Portugal means a generic European Portuguese transcription. The module has its own profiles named gpt (general Portugal), cpt (central) and spt (southern), but the app passes the code pt, which is not one of them. The module treats any style that is not Brazilian as "Portugal" and applies its extra regional rules only for exact style names: the lowering of ei to ɐj only for gpt, and the monophthongization of ei/ou only for spt. With pt neither fires, so the output equals that of cpt: for example beijo /ˈbej.ʒu/ keeps ej.

Limitations

  • Supported: Rio de Janeiro Portuguese (Brazil) and a generic European Portuguese (Portugal).
  • Not selectable: São Paulo, southern Brazilian, gpt-specific, southern Portuguese, African or Asian varieties, and every other regional accent.
  • No lexicon: loanwords, names and irregular words are transcribed from their spelling, so many need a respelling (see Common Issues).
  • One word at a time: the app splits your text at spaces and transcribes every word on its own, so rules that look across word boundaries (for example s becoming z before a vowel in the next word) never apply.
  • Only the first variant is shown: see Multiple Pronunciation Variants.
  • Two engine bugs make some ordinary words fail: Brazilian words ending in stressed -io(s) and Portuguese words ending in -le. See Common Issues.

Phonemic vs Phonetic

On this page /slashes/ mark a phonemic transcription and [square brackets] a phonetic one; the app itself prints the bare IPA without either symbol.

  • Phonemic: A simplified, broad transcription that shows only the sounds that are essential for distinguishing meaning (phonemes). It represents the abstract sound system of the language. Optional sounds are shown in parentheses, for example the optional final vowel in the Portugal transcription cidade /siˈda.d(ɨ)/.
  • Phonetic: A detailed, narrow transcription that shows subtle, non-distinctive variations in pronunciation (allophones). It aims to represent how a word is actually said. In the module, phonetic output is the phonemic output plus a short list of allophonic rules.

Both forms are produced by the same code path, only with a phonetic flag. The phonetic form differs from the phonemic form in exactly these ways:

Important Note: Why You Might See an Error

This transcription engine is designed not to guess. Because Portuguese spelling is not always perfectly phonetic, the engine will intentionally refuse to transcribe a word when the spelling leaves a sound undetermined. To fix this, you need to provide a "respelling" in the input box that clarifies the intended sound. The two most common reasons are described below.

1. Ambiguous Stressed Vowels (e and o)

This is the most common cause of errors. In Portuguese, a stressed e or o can be open (ɛ, ɔ) or closed (e, o), but standard spelling often does not use an accent mark to show which. There is no default: the engine does not pick one.

Example: The word governo has stress on the e, but the engine cannot know whether it is open or closed, so it fails: governo (error). The same happens for ele (error), hoje (error), mesa (error) and saber (error).

Solution: Add an accent mark to the stressed vowel to specify its quality.

  • For a closed sound, use a circumflex: govêrno gives govêrno /ɡoˈveʁ.nu/ in Brazil and govêrno /ɡuˈveɾ.nu/ in Portugal.
  • For an open sound, use an acute accent: féra gives féra /ˈfɛ.ɾɐ/ and féra /ˈfɛ.ɾɐ/; hóje gives hóje /ˈɔ.ʒi/ and hóje /ˈɔ.ʒ(ɨ)/.
When no accent is needed. The engine fills in the vowel quality by itself in several situations, so you only need to mark the cases that remain: a stressed e/o that is nasal (homem /ˈõ.mẽj̃/, tempo /ˈtẽ.pu/), part of a diphthong (leite /ˈlej.t͡ʃi/, pouco /ˈpo(w).ku/), in the endings -dor/-tor/-sor and -oso/-osa (invasor /ĩ.vaˈzoʁ/, gostoso /ɡoʃˈto.zu/, gostosa /ɡoʃˈtɔ.zɐ/), or in a hiatus (boa /ˈbo.ɐ/). A stressed a never needs a mark. See Open and Closed e / o.

Internally the error text is "Stressed e or o not occurring nasalized or in a diphthong must be marked for quality using é/ê or ó/ô". The app does not display that text; see below.

2. The Irregular Letter x

The letter x has several possible pronunciations (ʃ, z, ks, s), and in many positions the engine cannot reliably choose. It resolves x only in the cases listed in The Letter x (initial, final, after ai/ei/oi/ou/en, and before a consonant after e) and fails everywhere else.

Example: The engine fails on exame (error), táxi (error) and lixo (error), but succeeds on caixa /ˈkaj.ʃɐ/ (because x after ai is always ʃ) and on enxame /ẽˈʃɐ̃.mi/. The older version of this page listed caixa as a failing example; it does not fail.

Solution: Replace the x with its phonetic equivalent in the input.

  • For the z sound in exame, enter ezame: ezame /eˈzɐ̃.mi/ (Brazil), ezame /iˈzɐ.m(ɨ)/ (Portugal).
  • For the ks sound in táxi, enter tácsi: tácsi /ˈtak.si/, tácsi /ˈta.ksi/.
  • For the ʃ sound in lixo, enter lisho: lisho /ˈli.ʃu/.

The internal error text is "x must be respelled z, sh, cs, ss or similar".

3. What the App Shows When It Fails

When the module raises an error, the app catches it and returns your input word unchanged, marked with the error style (the word appears as plain, untranscribed text instead of IPA). The explanatory message above is written only to the browser's developer console. Nothing else on the line is affected: every other word is still transcribed.

4. What You Can (and Cannot) Type

Before transcription the app removes everything that is not a letter, a combining accent, an apostrophe or a hyphen. This matters for respellings, because the module has an extended respelling notation that therefore cannot be used in this app:

You type Works in the app? Notes
Accents á é í ó ú â ê ô ã õ ç ü Yes Precomposed and combining accents are both accepted. Capital letters are lowercased automatically.
Letter substitutions: z, sh, cs, ss, h Yes For example marh forces a final r to stay pronounced without parentheses (see R Sounds).
Hyphen - Yes Splits a word into components; each component gets its own stress and earlier ones become secondary stress: guarda-chuva /ˌɡwaʁ.dɐˈʃu.vɐ/.
Apostrophe ' Yes Becomes a tie mark: d'água /ˈd‿a.ɡwɐ/.
Grave accent / macron (à, ō) Portugal only In the module these mean "explicitly unstressed open / closed vowel" for Portugal only. In Brazil they are deleted, so sòlo fails in Brazil (stressed o unmarked) but gives sòlo /sɔ.lu/ in Portugal.
Dot ., ^, *, +, :, #, comma, digits, ?, ! No These are stripped before the engine sees them, so the module's syllable-forcing dot, optional-vowel markers (e^, i^) and component markers (+, :) are unavailable. ri.o becomes rio.

Quick Reference (Glossary)

Phoneme
The smallest unit of sound in a language that distinguishes meaning (e.g., /p/ vs. /b/ in "pata" vs. "bata").
Allophone
A variant pronunciation of a phoneme that doesn't change meaning (e.g., b vs. β in Portugal).
Respelling
An alternative spelling you type to tell the engine how a word is pronounced, for example govêrno for governo or ezame for exame. The module was built around respellings.
Open / Closed Vowel
Portuguese distinguishes open ɛ ɔ from closed e o in stressed syllables. Spelling marks them with an acute (é, ó) and a circumflex (ê, ô).
Oxytone / Paroxytone
A word stressed on its last syllable (feliz /feˈliʃ/) or on its second-to-last syllable (casa /ˈka.zɐ/).
Vowel Reduction
Weakening of unstressed vowels. In Portugal it is strong (e to ɨ, a to ɐ, o to u); in Brazil it is mostly limited to the word end (final e to i, o to u).
Schwa-like Vowel ɨ
A close central unrounded vowel; in this module the reduced form of unstressed e in Portugal. It is often dropped, which the output shows as (ɨ).
Nasal Vowel
A vowel pronounced with air also passing through the nose, marked with a tilde: ɐ̃ ẽ ĩ õ ũ. Spelled with ã/õ or with m/n after the vowel.
Nasal Diphthong
A nasal vowel followed by a nasal glide: ɐ̃w̃ (ão), ɐ̃j̃ (ãe), õj̃ (õe), ẽj̃ (-em).
Diphthong / Glide
A vowel plus a short non-syllabic j or w (written i/u) in the same syllable, e.g. pai /ˈpaj/, mau /ˈmaw/.
Hiatus
Two vowels in adjacent syllables with no consonant between them, e.g. saída /saˈi.dɐ/. In some contexts the second vowel may be pronounced as a glide.
Palatalization
Shift of a sound towards the hard palate; in Brazil t/d before i become t͡ʃ/d͡ʒ.
Affricate / Tie Bar
A stop released as a fricative, written with a tie bar over the two symbols: t͡ʃ, d͡ʒ.
Tap
A single quick tongue-tip flap, ɾ, as in the middle of caro /ˈka.ɾu/.
Strong R
The "rr" sound, ʁ in the phonemic form, as in carro /ˈka.ʁu/. In Rio it is phonetically the voiceless uvular fricative χ.
Chiado
The "hushing" pronunciation of syllable-final s/z as ʃ/ʒ, typical of Rio and of Portugal.
Velarized L
The dark ɫ that Portugal uses for coda l in the phonetic form; Brazil vocalizes it to w.
Fricativized Stop
In Portugal's phonetic form, b d g between vowels are pronounced β ð ɣ.
Epenthesis
Insertion of a sound. The Brazilian output inserts an optional (j) after a stressed vowel before final s/z, e.g. faz /ˈfa(j)ʃ/. (Epenthetic i inside consonant clusters is not inserted automatically.)
Coda / Onset
The final consonant(s) / initial consonant(s) of a syllable.
Syllable Boundary
The marker . placed between syllables in the output.
Primary / Secondary Stress
Marked ˈ and ˌ before the stressed syllable. Only the last stressed syllable of a word keeps primary stress; earlier ones (for example in compounds, prefixes and -mente adverbs) are downgraded to secondary.
Unstressed Word (Clitic)
A short word that carries no stress of its own: articles, many prepositions and object pronouns (o, a, de, que, me, se ...). The module has fixed lists of them and treats them with or without vowel reduction.
Optional Sound
A sound shown in parentheses, e.g. (j), (ɨ), (ʁ): it may or may not be pronounced.
NFD
Unicode Normalization Form Decomposed: letters with accents are split into base letter plus combining accent for processing (except ç and ü, which are re-composed).

Interactive Features

  • Audio playback: Click the speaker icon next to any word or line to hear text-to-speech pronunciation (requires browser TTS support). The Portuguese language entry is configured with the pt-BR voice code, so the audio does not change when you switch between the "Brazil" and "Portugal" options.
  • Export results: Use PDF or CSV buttons to save transcriptions in your preferred format.
  • No variant cycling: unlike German, Portuguese words never carry alternative IPA forms in the app, so clicking a word does not cycle anything (see below).

Multiple Pronunciation Variants

The module itself can return several pronunciations for one input, each labelled with a qualifier. For example, in Brazil the initial es- before a consonant can be i or e, initial em-/en- can be careful ẽ or natural ĩ, hiatus i/u can be a full vowel or a glide ("faster pronunciation"), and in Portugal -sC clusters have "careful" and "natural" forms.

The app displays only the first variant: the handler returns result[0]. For example, estar /iʃˈta(ʁ)/ shows the i-variant; the e-variant is not reachable. Likewise criança /kɾiˈɐ̃.sɐ/ shows the full-vowel variant, never the glide one.

Portuguese Pronunciation Guide

This guide explains the pronunciation rules that the engine implements. Portuguese spelling is fairly regular once you know where the stress falls and how open or closed the stressed vowel is, which is exactly the information the engine asks you to supply when it cannot determine it. Brazil and Portugal differ most in vowel reduction, nasality and a handful of consonants; the sections below show both side by side.

Spelling, Accent Marks and Respelling

In Portuguese the written accents do two jobs at once: they mark which syllable is stressed and they fix the vowel quality. The engine reads them in the same way, so you should put an accent only on the stressed vowel.

Acute á é ó Stressed open vowel (a ɛ ɔ) pára /ˈpa.ɾɐ/, pé /ˈpɛ/
Circumflex â ê ô Stressed closed vowel (ɐ e o) pâra /ˈpɐ.ɾɐ/, pê /ˈpe/
Tilde ã õ Nasal vowel; the last tilde syllable receives stress unless an accent says otherwise irmão /iʁˈmɐ̃w̃/, manhã /mɐ̃ˈɲɐ̃/

The module is case-insensitive: capitals are lowercased first, so names like Maria /maˈɾi.ɐ/ need no special treatment. Letters that are not part of Portuguese spelling pass through unchanged (for example w and k), which is why loanwords usually need a respelling, as explained under Common Issues.

Stress Patterns

Word stress is assigned after syllable division, in this order (the first rule that applies wins):

  1. An acute or circumflex accent marks the stressed syllable: lápis /ˈla.piʃ/, café /kaˈfɛ/, você /voˈse/.
  2. A tilde without an accent: the last syllable that contains a tilde is stressed: irmão /iʁˈmɐ̃w̃/, manhã /mɐ̃ˈɲɐ̃/, pão-de-ló /ˌpɐ̃w̃.d͡ʒiˈlɔ/ (the last component keeps primary stress).
  3. The default rule:
    • Words ending in -a, -e, -o (with or without -s), -am, -em, -ens or -ans are stressed on the second-to-last syllable (paroxytone): casa /ˈka.zɐ/, cidade /siˈda.d͡ʒi/, pastas /ˈpaʃ.tɐʃ/, cantam /ˈkɐ̃.tɐ̃w̃/, item /ˈi.tẽj̃/.
    • All other words are stressed on the last syllable (oxytone): feliz /feˈliʃ/, azul /aˈzuw/, cantar /kɐ̃ˈta(ʁ)/, jardim /ʒaʁˈd͡ʒĩ/, tatu /taˈtu/, aqui /aˈki/, algum /awˈɡũ/.

The default rule assumes the dictionary stress for regular words only: anything that breaks it (for example órgão, lápis, bênção) is written with an accent in standard orthography and the engine trusts that accent.

-mente, -zinho and Hyphenated Components

  • Adverbs in -mente and diminutives in -zinho/-zinha(s) are split into two parts and each part is stressed separately; the first becomes secondary stress: rapidamente /ʁaˌpi.daˈmẽ.t͡ʃi/, rapazinho /ˌʁa.paˈzĩ.ɲu/. A stressed e/o in the first part must be marked just as in the base word: certamente fails, cértamente works: cértamente /ˌsɛʁ.taˈmẽ.t͡ʃi/.
  • A hyphen splits a word into components that are stressed independently, and every primary stress except the last becomes secondary: guarda-chuva /ˌɡwaʁ.dɐˈʃu.vɐ/, bem-vindo /ˌbẽj̃ˈvĩ.du/.
  • A prefix typed with a trailing hyphen is treated as unstressed with vowel reduction (it has no accent): anti- /ɐ̃.t͡ʃi/.

Nasal Vowels and Nasal Diphthongs

Nasality is derived from spelling in a fixed order:

  1. Final unstressed -am (third-person plural verbs) is treated like -ão: cantam /ˈkɐ̃.tɐ̃w̃/, falam /ˈfa.lɐ̃w̃/.
  2. In Portugal a final -n is a real n and the unstressed vowel before it is open: hífen /ˈi.fɛn/ (compare Brazil hífen /ˈi.fẽj̃/).
  3. A vowel followed by m or n in the same syllable is nasalized and the consonant is not pronounced: tempo /ˈtẽ.pu/, lindo /ˈlĩ.du/, compra /ˈkõ.pɾɐ/, mundo /ˈmũ.du/.
  4. A non-high vowel nasalized this way gets a circumflex (closed): canto /ˈkɐ̃.tu/, tempo /ˈtẽ.pu/.
  5. In Brazil a stressed vowel before m/n in the next syllable is also nasalized: cama /ˈkɐ̃.mɐ/ ano /ˈɐ̃.nu/; all vowels before nh are nasalized and, in the phonetic form, nh becomes j̃: banho /ˈbɐ̃.ɲu/ banho [ˈbɐ̃.j̃u]. Portugal only closes the vowel: cama /ˈkɐ.mɐ/, banho /ˈbɐ.ɲu/.
  6. ão, ãe, õe, and -ui in muit- are nasal diphthongs ending in a nasal glide: pão /ˈpɐ̃w̃/, mãe /ˈmɐ̃j̃/, põe /ˈpõj̃/, muito /ˈmũj̃.tu/, limões /liˈmõj̃ʃ/.
  7. Final -em and -ens are ẽj̃(s): bem /ˈbẽj̃/, homem /ˈõ.mẽj̃/, parabéns /pa.ɾaˈbẽj̃ʃ/.
  8. In Portugal a circumflex on final -em (vêm, têm, mantêm) signals the special double nasal ending: têm /ˈtẽj̃.ẽj̃/ versus têm /ˈtẽj̃/; and põem gets a hiatus: põem /ˈpõj̃.ẽj̃/ versus põem /ˈpõj̃/.
  9. In Brazil an initial unstressed em-/en- before a consonant is ẽ (careful variant, shown) or ĩ: enxame /ẽˈʃɐ̃.mi/.

S and Z

  • Initial s, ss, ç, c before e/i, sc: s: sapo /ˈsa.pu/, passa /ˈpa.sɐ/, ça /ˈsa/. After a nasal vowel s stays voiceless: pensar /pẽˈsa(ʁ)/.
  • Between vowels (or before a voiced consonant): z: casa /ˈka.zɐ/. An exception is trans- + vowel: transar /tɾɐ̃ˈza(ʁ)/.
  • Coda s/z before a consonant or at the end of the word: in Rio and in Portugal it becomes ʃ (voiceless context) or ʒ (before a voiced consonant); it stays s/z only in word-initial position: pasta /ˈpaʃ.tɐ/, asma /ˈaʒ.mɐ/, casas /ˈka.zɐʃ/, feliz /feˈliʃ/.
  • Brazilian epenthetic (j): after a stressed oral vowel (not i) before final s/z the Rio output inserts an optional glide: luz /ˈlu(j)ʃ/, mês /ˈme(j)ʃ/, faz /ˈfa(j)ʃ/, but not after i (feliz /feˈliʃ/), not in nasal vowels or diphthongs, and not in unstressed words (mas /maʃ/, os /uʃ/). Portugal has none: luz /ˈluʃ/.
  • Two sibilants in a row reduce to one in Rio: desc-, nasc- give nascêr /naˈse(ʁ)/; in Portugal the first variant keeps both (ʃs): nascêr /nɐʃˈseɾ/.
  • ex + consonant: s in Brazil but ejʃ in Portugal: excelente /e.seˈlẽ.t͡ʃi/ / excelente /ejʃ.sɨˈlẽ.t(ɨ)/, têxtil /ˈteʃ.t͡ʃiw/ / têxtil /ˈtejʃ.til/.

A word-initial s followed by a consonant is not shushed: spray /ˈspɾaj/, slide /ˈsli.d͡ʒi/.

R Sounds

  • Strong r ʁ: written rr, word-initial r, and r after n, l, s, z (and after a nasal vowel): carro /ˈka.ʁu/, rato /ˈʁa.tu/, honra /ˈõ.ʁɐ/, genro /ˈʒẽ.ʁu/.
  • Tap ɾ: r between vowels or in a cluster after a consonant: caro /ˈka.ɾu/, prato /ˈpɾa.tu/.
  • Coda r (before a consonant or at the end): Brazil (Rio) gives ʁ phonemically; Portugal gives the tap ɾ: pórta /ˈpɔʁ.tɐ/, pórta /ˈpɔɾ.tɐ/.
  • Rio phonetic form: strong r is χ, or ʁ when a voiced consonant follows: rua [ˈχu.ɐ], pórta [ˈpɔχ.tɐ], bórdo [ˈbɔʁ.du].
  • Final r after a stressed a, ɛ, e, i (infinitives, but also mar) is shown optional in Brazil, because it is often dropped: falar /faˈla(ʁ)/, partir /paʁˈt͡ʃi(ʁ)/, mar /ˈma(ʁ)/. Respell with a silent h to keep it fixed: marh /ˈmaʁ/. After other vowels it is always written: pôr /ˈpoʁ/. Portugal keeps the tap: falar /fɐˈlaɾ/.

  • Onset l is l in both dialects: lá /ˈla/.
  • lh is ʎ: filho /ˈfi.ʎu/.

Syllable Division

Syllables are divided before stress is assigned, because stress counts syllables. The rules:

  • The division goes before the last consonant between two vowels, then moves left over a stop (p b t d k g f v) + l/r cluster, which can start a syllable: prato /ˈpɾa.tu/, compra /ˈkõ.pɾɐ/.
  • Brazil divides every other consonant cluster between the consonants; Portugal keeps "improper" clusters of non-sibilant obstruent + obstruent or nasal together: ritmo /ˈʁit.mu/ versus ritmo /ˈʁi.tmu/, técnica /ˈtɛk.ni.kɐ/ versus técnica /ˈtɛ.kni.kɐ/, advogado /ad.voˈɡa.du/ versus advogado /ɐ.dvuˈɡa.du/.
  • Two vowels are split into two syllables, except a/e/o + unstressed i/u (diphthongs: saudade /sawˈda.d͡ʒi/, peixe /ˈpe(j).ʃi/), final -iu(s), -ui(s), and the nasal diphthongs ão, ãe, õe.
  • Even such a diphthong is split before m, n, l, r or nh (sair, Bombaim, Coimbra, rainha): sair /saˈi(ʁ)/, bombaim /bõ.baˈĩ/; compare baile /ˈbaj.li/ where the l belongs to the next syllable and the diphthong survives.
  • Brazil keeps written dj as one affricate sound except after initial a (ad.j): adjetivo /ad.ʒeˈt͡ʃi.vu/.

Using the Tool: Practical Notes

Should I choose Brazil or Portugal?

Choose Brazil for Rio de Janeiro pronunciation and Portugal for a generic European transcription. Other regional accents, such as São Paulo or southern Brazil, are not selectable; see Dialects & Limitations and the comparison in Full Dialect Comparison.

What is the difference between the Phonemic and Phonetic forms?

The phonemic form is the broad transcription; the phonetic form adds a short list of allophonic rules. For Brazil it changes the strong r, as in rato /ˈʁa.tu/ against [ˈχa.tu]; for Portugal it turns b, d, g into fricatives, as in cidade /siˈda.d(ɨ)/ against [siˈða.ð(ɨ)]. See Phonemic vs Phonetic.

Why does a word come back unchanged, and how do I respell it?

The engine refuses a stressed e or o whose quality is not marked, and most uses of x. Mark the stressed vowel with é/ê or ó/ô: governo is returned unchanged, but govêrno gives /ɡoˈveʁ.nu/ in Brazil and /ɡuˈveɾ.nu/ in Portugal. Replace x by its sound, for example exame as ezame. A few regular words, such as Brazilian rio, fail because of a module bug; all of this is explained in Why You Might See an Error. Open in the converter

Why can the output differ from a dictionary?

The tool has no lexicon: each word is transcribed from its spelling alone, one space-separated word at a time. Loanwords and irregular spellings therefore need a respelling, for example pizza gives /ˈpi.zɐ/ while pítsa gives /ˈpit.sɐ/ in Brazil. See Common Issues & Limitations.

Other Limitations

  • No stress or quality inference for e/o: by design the engine fails rather than guessing.
  • Only the first variant is shown (see Multiple Pronunciation Variants).
  • Punctuation and digits are removed before transcription; a word consisting only of such characters comes back as an error.
  • Respelling notation (^, ., +, :, *) cannot be typed, so optional vowels, forced syllable boundaries and component markers are not available.
  • Portugal is plain pt: the module's general-Portugal rules (gpt) are not active.
